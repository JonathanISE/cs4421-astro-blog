In How Countries Go Broke, Ray Dalio sets out what he calls the Big Debt Cycle: how governments borrow, how central banks print money to keep the borrowing going, and how that cycle eventually ends in a debt crisis. He backs it up with history, looking at how this has played out for countries over the centuries, before turning to the debt problems facing the United States and other big economies today.

What I liked most is how clearly he explains a subject that's usually buried in jargon; once you've read it, the news about deficits and interest rates makes a lot more sense. His explanation of why it keeps happening is uncomfortably human, too: we do it "because it is more enjoyable to borrow and spend." Dalio himself is the star of the show (yes, I made him my favourite character), and his decades of running one of the world's biggest investment funds give the book a practical, real-world feel. He doesn't just warn about the problem, either; he suggests how a country could get its deficit down to around 3% of its economy before it's too late.

It isn't a light read. There are a lot of charts, the main ideas are repeated often, and it leans heavily on Dalio's own way of seeing the world, so it's worth reading other views alongside it. Still, if you've ever wondered how a whole country can run out of money, or want to understand the economic storms that might be coming, this is a Must Read.
